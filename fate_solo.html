Playing Fate Solo: Be Your Own GM

Oracles, scene checks and self-compels with nothing but Fate dice

Why Fate Works Alone

Fate Core is written for a group with a GM, and it has no official solo rules. Yet it is one of the friendliest systems to play by yourself. Aspects are ready-made story prompts, the four outcomes already include "success at a cost," and fate points reward you for making trouble for your own character. All a solo player really needs to add is something to answer the questions a GM would normally answer. That something is an oracle.

The Writers' Room of One

Earlier parts compared a Fate table to a television writers' room. Playing solo, you're the whole room: player, showrunner and the voice that says "what if it goes wrong?" The oracle is that last voice. You ask it the questions you can't fairly answer yourself, and it keeps the story surprising.

Your Solo Kit

You don't need anything Fate doesn't already use:

  • Four Fate dice (or the dice roller). They run both your actions and the oracle.
  • One character. Build them with character creation. Fate Accelerated or Fate Condensed are good lighter options if you want less bookkeeping.
  • Index cards or a notes file for situation aspects, NPCs and open questions.
  • A journal. Writing the story down is half the fun of solo play, and it's how you remember threads between sessions.

💡 Tip: Dedicated GM emulators such as Mythic Game Master Emulator (Word Mill Games) work well with Fate too. This part sticks to an oracle built from Fate's own dice, so you can start without buying anything. For solo play in general, see Ray's Solo RPG Mastery guide.

The Fate Dice Oracle

The oracle borrows Fate's own outcome ladder. Ask a yes/no question you genuinely don't know the answer to, decide how likely "yes" is, and roll 4dF against the matching difficulty. Read the result the way you'd read any Fate roll:

  • Beat it by 3 or more (succeed with style) → Yes, and… Yes, plus something extra in your favor.
  • Beat it by 1–2 (succeed) → Yes.
  • Tie → Yes, but… Yes, with a complication or cost.
  • Miss by 1–2 (fail) → No.
  • Miss by 3 or more → No, and… No, and things get worse.

Setting the Odds

Pick the difficulty from how likely the answer seems in the fiction. These are the exact odds with four Fate dice (rounded, so rows may not add to exactly 100%):

LikelihoodDifficultyNo, and…NoYes, but…YesYes, and…
Very likely−20%6%12%43%38%
Likely−11%17%20%43%19%
Toss-up+06%32%23%32%6%
Unlikely+119%43%20%17%1%
Very unlikely+238%43%12%6%0%

Notice that a toss-up leans slightly toward yes, because a tie counts as "Yes, but…". That's deliberate: complications keep a solo story moving, while a flat "no" can stall it.

Running Scenes Solo

A solo session is a chain of scenes. Each one starts with what you expect to happen and ends when its question is answered.

graph TD A[Frame the scene you expect] --> B{Scene check} B -->|As expected| C[Play the scene] B -->|Altered| D[Change one detail<br/>then play] B -->|Interrupted| E[Something else happens<br/>use a random spark] D --> C E --> C C --> F[Ask the oracle when unsure] F --> G[Roll skills for actions] G --> H[Note new aspects and threads] H --> A

The Scene Check

Before each scene, decide what you expect to happen, then roll 4dF once. This is a simple house rule inspired by GM emulators, not a Fate Core rule:

  • −3 or −4 (about 6%): Interrupted. Something different happens. Use a random spark or your trouble aspect for inspiration.
  • −1 or −2 (about 32%): Altered. The scene happens, but one detail is different: someone extra is there, the location has changed, or the timing is off.
  • 0 or higher (about 62%): As expected. Play it as you imagined.

Situation Aspects Do the Heavy Lifting

Give every scene two or three situation aspects, just as a GM would ("Rain-Slick Rooftops", "Too Many Witnesses"). When you're unsure what something looks like, ask the oracle about an aspect instead of inventing everything. "Is the alley Dimly Lit?" is a perfect oracle question, and a "yes" gives you an aspect to invoke later.

Running the Opposition

The hardest part of solo play is staying fair to the other side. Fate gives you tools that make this easier.

Pick Difficulties from the Ladder

Use passive opposition the way Fate Core suggests: Fair (+2) for an ordinary obstacle, Good (+3) or Great (+4) for something your character should struggle with, and Mediocre (+0) or Average (+1) for things that are only hard because of time pressure. Decide the number before you roll, and don't change it after.

Let NPCs Roll for Themselves

When an NPC actively opposes you, roll 4dF and add their skill, exactly as a GM would. Rough guides from Fate Core's NPC types:

  • Nameless NPCs (thugs, guards): one or two skills at Average (+1) or Fair (+2), and little or no stress.
  • Supporting NPCs: a few skills up to Good (+3), a couple of aspects and a mild consequence.
  • Main NPCs: built like a player character, often with a peak skill matching or exceeding yours.

Let the Oracle Decide What NPCs Do

When you're unsure how an NPC reacts, ask: "Does the guard believe me?" "Does she attack first?" Set the likelihood from their aspects. A character with Paranoid About Everything makes "Does he trust me?" Unlikely or Very unlikely.

Conflicts, Contests and Challenges

These work exactly as written. Roll for both sides, track stress and consequences honestly, and let concessions happen. Playing solo, it can be tempting to fight to the last box; conceding early to keep a story going is often the better choice.

Fate Points with One Player

The fate point economy is what makes Fate feel like Fate, so keep it running even when you're alone.

  • Start each session at your refresh (or keep any extra points from last session, as Fate Core allows).
  • Compel yourself. When your trouble or another aspect would naturally make life harder, accept the complication and take a fate point. If you'd rather avoid it, pay a fate point to refuse, just as at a table.
  • Use the oracle for compels. If you aren't sure an aspect should complicate things, ask: "Does my Can't Let Go of Unsolved Mysteries pull me off course here?"
  • Give the opposition a pool. In Fate Core the GM starts each scene with fate points equal to the number of PCs. Solo, that's one per scene. Spend it on main NPCs' aspects when it makes the story more interesting.

⚠️ Watch out: The most common solo mistake is never compelling yourself. If your fate points only ever go down, you're playing it too safe. Lean into your trouble.

🛠️ How To: Your First Solo Session

  1. Make one character. High concept, trouble, three more aspects, a skill pyramid and a few stunts. Keep it simple.
  2. Write a one-line problem. "Someone is stealing artifacts using my research." Turn it into an aspect for the session.
  3. Frame the opening scene. Where are you, what do you want right now, and what's in the way? Add two situation aspects.
  4. Make a scene check. Adjust the scene if it's altered or interrupted.
  5. Play. Roll skills for actions, ask the oracle for unknowns, roll for NPCs who push back.
  6. Close the scene when its question is answered. Note new aspects, NPCs and open questions.
  7. Repeat until the session's problem is resolved or you reach a good cliffhanger. Then write a short journal entry.

Example of Play

Dr. Rita Anderson is chasing whoever is using her research to find artifacts.

Scene: Rita expects to search a rival's office after hours. Scene check: −1, Altered. She decides the office isn't empty: a cleaner is still working.

Question: "Does the cleaner recognize me?" Rita has given talks here, so it's Likely (difficulty −1). Roll: +0, beating it by 1: Yes.

Action: Rita tries to talk her way in with Rapport against the cleaner's Will, which Rita sets at Average (+1). She rolls +2 on top of Rapport Good (+3) for Superb (+5), beating the cleaner by 4: success with style. The cleaner lets her in and mentions that "the American with the old maps" was here yesterday, which becomes a new situation aspect, A Visitor With Old Maps, with a free invoke.

Compel: Searching the desk, Rita finds her own stolen notes. Her trouble, Can't Let Go of Unsolved Mysteries, says she stays to read them instead of leaving quietly. She accepts the compel and takes a fate point, and security walks in a minute later.

Common Questions

Isn't it boring to know everything already?

You won't. The oracle, scene checks and NPC rolls answer the questions you'd normally leave to a GM, so you're discovering the story as you play. Only decide what you would decide as a player.

What if the oracle gives an answer that makes no sense?

Reinterpret it before you reroll. A "yes" that seems impossible often hides a twist. If it truly breaks the story, it's fine to treat it as "Yes, but…" or ask a smaller question.

Should I play one character or a group?

Start with one. Two or three characters can work, but every extra character adds bookkeeping. A single character with strong relationships to NPCs is usually enough.

How do I avoid "fudging" for my character?

Set difficulties and NPC skills before you roll, roll in the open (even if only you can see), and honor concessions and consequences. When in doubt, ask the oracle rather than choosing.

Key Takeaways

  • Fate has no official solo rules, but its aspects and outcomes make it easy to play alone with a simple oracle.
  • The Fate dice oracle uses the familiar ladder: set a difficulty from how likely "yes" is, then read fail, tie, succeed or succeed with style.
  • Scene checks and random sparks stop you from writing a story you already know.
  • Decide difficulties and NPC skills before rolling, and let NPCs roll for themselves.
  • Keep the fate point economy alive by compelling your own aspects.

📓 Learning Journal

After your first solo session, jot down:

  • Which oracle answer surprised you most, and what it changed?
  • Did you compel yourself? If not, where could you have?
  • Were any difficulties too easy or too hard?
  • What open questions or threads do you want to pick up next time?
